**Authors:** Juan Valentin Guerrero Cano, Gabriel Jonas Aguiar, Alberto Cano

**Venue:** Machine Learning (2026) 115:3 — DOI: 10.1007/s10994-025-06945-4 (Gold Open Access)

*Received 10 June 2025 / Published online 14 December 2025*

## Abstract

Adapting to drifting data streams remains a key challenge in online learning, where effec tive model adaptation depends on timely concept drift detection. Most existing approaches respond to drift only after distributional changes occur, reacting to concept drift, limiting their ability to prevent the classifier’s performance degradation. This work introduces a novel methodology to anticipate concept drift and enable proactive adaptation before the data distribution shift negatively impacts the classifier. We propose four proactive ad aptation strategies based on the Very Fast Decision Tree (VFDT) algorithm to leverage data trends to estimate proactive changes in the classifier, mitigating or even preventing the performance degradation consequence of the concept drift. We evaluate the proposed methods across four scenarios with diverse data stream configurations. Results demon strate that proactive adaptation reduces the adverse effects of concept drift and improves classification performance. In particular, the proposed strategies consistently outperformed in settings with incremental drift, underscoring the potential of anticipatory approaches and addressing a notable gap in the current literature. Keywords Machine learning · Data streams · Concept drift · Proactive learning

## 1 Introduction

The astonishing velocity at which data is generated nowadays has substantially increased over the past decades. The information generated by worldwide communications, social media, the Internet of Things, and other innovative technologies is often handled through continuous frameworks, due to its potentially infinite character (Chen and Zhang, 2014; Isah et al., 2019). Traditional machine learning models, which are mostly trained in static and batch data, struggle to learn from continuous and evolving information sources, commonly referred to as streams. The static assumptions on which these models are based, prevent them from effectively capturing the information these dynamic data streams provide. The principal feature of data streams is their evolving nature, as the probabilistic dis tribution underlying the continuous arriving information may change over time. This phe nomenon, known as concept drift, poses one of the main challenges in the field of data streams (Agrahari and Singh, 2022; Khamassi et al., 2018). Concept drift can significantly affect model predictions, making the learned knowledge obsolete. Research community has proposed several methodologies to address the challenges induced by concept drift. The most prevalent approach involves detection mechanisms (Agrahari and Singh, 2022; Ditzler et al., 2015), which rely on triggering signals to adjust the model to the detected concept drift. Conversely, implicit mechanisms do not wait for a specific signal, but naturally understand the underlying change on the data distribution concept drifts cause. However, traditional approaches only considered reacting to concept drift rather than anticipating and early adapting to it (Gama et al., 2014). Although these methods offer robust and optimal results for most concept drifts scenarios, they still present opportuni ties for improvement. By merely reacting to concept drift, the models require significant enough evidence to confirm changes in the data distribution before adjusting their parame ters accordingly. Consequently, this evidence often leads to performance degradation, as for a certain period of the data stream, the model is obsolete, relying on outdated knowledge. In this context, estimating future data distribution trends may help to anticipate, or at least mitigate, the impact of concept drift. This work proposes a formal definition of such future estimation to mitigate and antici pate concept drift, which we define as proactive model adjustment. The main contributions of this paper are: ● Definition of proactive model adaptation: We comprehensively define reactive and proactive model adaptation to categorize how methods address concept drifts. This categorization offers a novel and powerful perspective on managing concept drift proactively and its potential benefits. ● Proactive version of VFDT: We propose a proactive version of the Very Fast Decision Tree (VFDT) by leveraging different proactive adaptation strategies to modify VFDT architecture as an example of the advantages of proactive models. ● Experimental comparison between proactive and reactive models: We conduct a study to analyze how different proactive strategies can perform across various concept drifts, benchmarking against a natural reactive baseline, VFDT. ● Recommendations and open challenges: We provide a comprehensive analysis of the advantages and limitations of proactive approaches, offering suggestions to build proactive adaptation strategies. We also outline potential research directions for future investigations into proactivity in data streams. This paper is organized as follows: Sect. 2 provides the foundational knowledge necessary to understand the current approaches in data stream, discussing various methodologies for handling concept drift. Section 3 presents the definition of reactive and proactive model adjustments. Section 4 details the methodology of the empirical study, explaining in detail the proactive strategies compared. Section 5 describes the experimental setup and evalu ation metrics. Section 6 presents and discusses the experimental results. Finally, Sects. 7 and 8 provide a comprehensive summary of the work, including our recommendations and future research challenges.

## 2 Background and Related Work

Data streams are potentially infinite sequences of data instances that arrive over time, often with high velocity. Formally, we define a data stream D as an infinite sequence of subsets D = {S 1,S 2,...S ∞} where each subset Si is received at time i. Depending on the learning setting, each subset Si may consist of a single instance (referred to as online data stream learning) or multiple instances (a batch data stream learning scenario). In supervised data stream learning, each instance is of the form (x, y), where y is a label. In unsupervised data stream learning, the labels are absent (Agrahari and Singh, 2022; Lu et al., 2018; Aguiar et al., 2024). Each subset of the data stream is drawn from a time-dependent probability distribution, denoted as Dt, where t represents discrete time steps. In a stationary data stream, this distribution remains constant over the time; otherwise, the stream is considered non-stationary. Specifically, Dt represents the joint distribution of the random variables X (feature space) and Y (label space), i.e. D t ∼ Pt(X, Y). In the context of data streams, Dt is referred to as the concept at time t. Most real-world data streams are non-stationary, meaning the underlying distribution can change over time. Formally, there may exist some t∗ ∈ N :D t∗ ̸= D t∗ +1. This phe nomenon is known as concept drifts and stands as one of the main challenges in data stream learning (Agrahari and Singh, 2022; Gama et al., 2014; Aguiar and Cano, 2024). From a probabilistic perspective, Dt denotes the distribution at time t. By the chain rule, we can factorize the joint distribution as: Pt(X, Y)= Pt(X )Pt(Y | X ) (1) Although this notation follows a discrete-style convention, both X and Y can be discrete or continuous in practice. The same decomposition applies in the continuous case, with Pt(·) interpreted as a probability density function. Concept drift can be categorized according to several characteristics, including its impact on decision boundaries, the speed of change, its locality, and its recurrence pattern (Agrahari and Singh, 2022; Aguiar et al., 2024): Influence of the decision boundaries. Focuses on how concept drift affects the classi fier’s decision boundaries. Specifically, we can distinguish between drifts that alter poste rior probabilities versus those that only affect the feature distribution. Real concept drift , that occurs when the posterior probability P (Y = yi | X ) changes, which directly shifts the decision boundaries. This typically leads to performance degradation unless the model adapts accordingly. Virtual concept drift, which occurs when the distribution of the input features P(X) changes while P (Y = yi | X ) remains constant. In such cases, although the distribution of features evolves, the decision boundaries may remain effective, since the underlying feature-label relationship is preserved. Locality. As defined by Khamassi et al. ( 2018), Aguiar and Cano ( 2024), locality considers two aspects: whether the concept drift affects a single class or multiple classes, and whether the drift impacts only a portion of a class distribution ( local) or the entire class (global). Based on these aspects, four types of drift can be identified: Single-class Local concept drift, when only one class is partially affected by the drift; Single-class Global concept drift where the entire distribution of one class is affected; Multi-Class Local concept drift, when multiple classes are each partially affected; and Multi-Class Global concept drift, where the whole distribution of multiple classes are impacted by the concept drift. Recurrence. This characteristic describes whether previously observed concepts reap pear over time. Recurrence can be classified as: Acyclic, when there is no specific periodicity for the recurrence of a previous concept, resulting in uncertainty about when concept may reappear; and Cyclic, when the recurrence of previous concepts follows a predictable pattern or periodic cycle, indicating when concept drift is likely to occur again. As a consequence of the concept drift, the knowledge acquired by a model may become outdated, leading to increased error rates and reduced performance. To address this chal lenge, it is essential to implement adaptation mechanisms that allow the model to adjust in response to distribution changes, and maintain stable performance over time. In the literature, several categorizations of adaptation mechanisms have been proposed, often sharing similar ideas with slight variations in interpretation. Common distinctions include active vs. passive approaches (Ditzler et al., 2015; Elwell and Polikar, 2011) where active methods rely on explicit drift detectors to trigger adaptation, while passive ones continuously update under the assumption that change may occur. Another widely used clas sification is blind vs. informed adaptation (Khamassi et al., 2018; Gama et al., 2014; Gomes et al., 2017a), where blind strategies adjust the model without detecting drift, whereas informed ones require a trigger. More recently, a distinction that has gained attention differentiates between explicit and implicit adaptation mechanisms (Aguiar et al., 2024): Explicit adaptation depends on external detectors that signal when to adapt, while implicit adapta tion relies on mechanisms embedded in the classifier itself. These categorizations are not mutually exclusive, and their strong conceptual overlap can lead to ambiguity when trying to differentiate them. In this work, we focus on the explicit versus implicit distinction, as it provides a structured and clear framework for analyzing how models handle concept drift. Implicit Adaptation. Implicit adaptation relies on the internal update mechanism of the learning algorithm to continuously adjust model structure or parameters without requiring an explicit drift signal. These methods typically employ incremental updating strategies that adapt with every new instance to changes in the underlying distribution. A representative example is the Concept-Adapting Very Fast Decision Tree (CVFDT)(Hulten et al., 2001). CVFDT extends the original VFDT algorithm by introducing a sliding window mechanism to handle concept drift. As new instances arrive, older ones are gradually forgotten, enabling the model to focus on the most recent data. Internally, CVFDT maintains alternate subtrees for parts of the model where recent data suggest a different splitting criterion might perform better. When an alternate subtree demonstrates superior performance over time, it replaces the original subtree, allowing the model to adapt to evolving concepts without requiring an explicit drift signal. Explicit Adaptation. Explicit methods depend on external mechanisms that detect concept drift and trigger model adaptation. Typically, a detection system signals when distri butional changes occur, prompting an associated adaptation strategy. These approaches are especially valuable when drift characterization is required (Khamassi et al., 2018). As highlighted in Ditzler et al. ( 2015), Lu et al. ( 2018), a broad range of detectors and strategies fall under this category. Most of these techniques follow a structured framework: data retrieval, data modeling, compute test statistics, and apply hypothesis tests to determine drift (Lu et al., 2018). These detectors are commonly grouped as follows. ● Error rate-based detectors monitor performance metrics such as classification error, raising alarms when statistically significant deviations are observed. For example, Drift Detection Method (DDM) (Gama et al., 2004) tracks the error over a sliding window and triggers adaptation when degradation crosses the warning and alarm thresholds. ADaptative WINdowing (ADWIN) (Bifet and Gavalda, 2007) compares statistics across dynamic window segments and reduces its size when significant changes are detected. These methods are typically supervised, as they rely on labeled data to measure performance. ● Data distribution-based detectors assess changes in the underlying data distribution directly, without relying on model performance. They apply dissimilarity metrics and hypothesis testing between historical and incoming data to detect drift in the distribu tion (Lu et al., 2018). Notable examples include Statistical Change Detection for multidimension data (SCD) (Song et al., 2007), Competence Model-based drift detection (CM) (Lu et al., 2016), PCA-based Change Detection framework (PCA-CD) (Qahtan et al., 2015) and Least Squares Density Difference-based Change Detection Test (LSDD- CDT) (Bu et al., 2016). These are often referred to as unsupervised detectors. ● Multiple hypothesis testing frameworks combine several tests either in parallel (e.g., Just-In-Time adaptive classifiers (JIT) (Alippi and Roveri, 2008), Linear Four Rate drift detection (LFR) (Wang and Abraham, 2015), Information Value and Jaccard similarity (IV-Jac) (Zhang et al., 2017)) or hierarchically (e.g., Hierarchical Change-Detection Tests (HCDTs) (Alippi et al., 2016), Hierarchical Linear Four Rate (HLFR) (Yu and Abraham, 2017) or Two-Stage Shift-Detection Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (TSMDS-EWMA) (Raza et al., 2015)). These methodologies increase robustness and reduce false positives by integrating multiple evidence sources. Concept drift adaptation, also referred to as drift reaction, encompasses methods designed to update the model in response to detected changes in the data distribution, ensuring con tinued predictive accuracy. According to Lu et al. (2018), these adaptation strategies can be grouped into three main categories: ● Simple retraining: When drift is detected, the model is retrained from scratch using recent data. For example, Paired Learners (Bach and Maloof, 2008) maintains two models: a stable learner optimized for long-term performance and a reactive learner tuned to short-term changes. When the reactive learner consistently outperforms the stable one, it replaces it, thus adapting to the new concept. ● Ensemble retraining: Instead of discarding all past models, ensemble methods pre serve and combine models trained across different concepts. Upon drift detection, a new model is trained and added to the ensemble, often replacing outdated ones. Leveraging Bagging (Bifet et al., 2010), combines bagging with ADWIN-based drift detection to replace underperforming models. Adaptive Random Forest (ARF) (Gomes et al., 2017b) extends the traditional random forest algorithm by using ADWIN to determine when outdated trees should be replaced. ● Model adjusting: Rather than retraining from scratch, some algorithms incrementally adjust internal components to adapt. As mentioned before, CVFDT (Hulten et al., 2001) extends VFDT by incorporating alternate subtrees and a sliding window to adapt structure over time. The Hoeffding Adaptive Tree (HAT) (Bifet and Gavalda, 2009) represents a more sophisticated evolution of the VFDT family, designed to handle concept drift through localized adaptation mechanisms. HAT incorporates ADWIN drift detec tors at each internal node to continuously monitor the quality of splitting attributes by tracking the accuracy of predictions made by the subtree rooted at that node. When a drift detector signals a significant change in performance, HAT identifies the affect ed node and adaptively prunes the corresponding subtree, replacing it with a new one trained on recent data. This node-level adaptation allows HAT to respond to localized concept changes while preserving stable parts of the tree structure, making it particu larly effective for handling drift that affects only specific regions of the feature space. While both implicit and explicit methods offer viable solutions for handling concept drift, each comes with its own advantages and limitations. The selection of an approach depends on factors such as drift speed, computational constraints, and adaptation requirements. Below, we summarize the main challenges associated with these methodologies. Limitations of Implicit Methods. Implicit methods are generally less popular than explicit ones due to their limitations, primarily their slow adaptation to concept drift. Since they update the model at a constant rate, regardless of the drift characteristics (e.g., abrupt or high-speed gradual drift), they struggle with sudden changes. Moreover, they assume a continuously evolving environment, leading to frequent model updates that may be computationally expensive or inefficient, especially when dealing with data streams where samples arrive rapidly or the stream remains mostly stationary. Methods such as VFDT rely on sta tistical indicators to update; however, this often results in error accumulation and delayed adaptation. Despite these drawbacks, implicit methods tend to perform well under gradual and low-speed drifts. Their effectiveness also depends on careful parameter tuning to bal ance stability and sensitivity (Gama et al., 2014), requiring a tradeoff to optimize perfor mance in different drift scenarios. Limitations of Explicit Methods. Explicit methods rely on triggering adaptation upon drift detection, but their effectiveness depends heavily on the chosen detection strategy. Most triggers are based on model error rates or data distribution changes, both prone to issues. A key challenge is the trade-off between false alarms and missed detections: The former can lead to unnecessary model updates and increased overhead, while the latter delay adaptation and harm performance. Therefore, explicit methods require careful calibration to balance sensitivity and specificity (Khamassi et al., 2018). Therefore, both explicit and implicit methods can be regarded as reactive by nature, although in different ways. Explicit methods wait for a detection signal before adapting, while implicit methods update continuously based on accumulated evidence (e.g., recent performance or distributional changes). In both cases, adaptation occurs only after the drift has manifested, rather than in anticipation of it. Several authors have explicitly classified these methodologies as reactive approaches (Khamassi et al., 2018; Ditzler et al., 2015; Han et al., 2022). Gama et al. (2014) argue that concept drift is naturally unpredictable, making its detection and handling pre dominantly reactive. Similarly, Aguiar et al. ( 2024) highlight this as an open challenge, noting that current adaptation techniques, particularly for imbalanced data and concept drift scenarios, still depend on changes in the data distribution having already occurred before adapting. In contrast to reactive approaches, several studies have explored “proactive” strategies for handling concept drift. However, most of these works focus mainly on anticipating drift through detection mechanisms (drift prediction) rather than directly adapting the model before drift manifests (Gunasekara et al., 2024; Escovedo et al., 2018). One of the earli est proactive approaches in data stream learning was proposed by Yang et al. (2006), who introduced RePro, a hybrid framework that combines reactive and proactive methodologies to handle recurring concept drift. RePro’s reactive component builds a history of observed concepts through standard drift detection and continuously monitors for recurring patterns in previously encountered concepts. Its proactive component learns transition patterns between concepts using Markov Chains, allowing the model to anticipate and prepare for future drifts when similar patterns reappear. However, RePro is specifically constrained to scenarios with clear recurring patterns and requires sufficient historical data to build reliable transition models. Similarly, Reactive and Proactive Concept Drift detection Ensemble (RePCoDE) (Ang et al., 2010) addresses concept drift in P2P networks by combining reactive and proactive adaptation strategies. RePCoDE’s proactive component identifies peers whose historical drift patterns resemble the local peer’s current trend through sequence similarity matching. Then, it selects and adapts models from these peers that represent post-drift concepts as predictors of future data. This enables proactive model adaptation before the local drift fully manifests, though the approach is limited to P2P environments and requires coordination between distributed peers, restricting its applicability to centralized learning scenarios. More recently, Chen et al. ( 2016) introduced ProSeed, which represents a shift toward volatility pattern-based proactive approaches. ProSeed enhances drift detection by incorporating historical drift rate information through probabilistic networks that model stream volatility patterns. Building upon Seed drift detector (Huang et al., 2014), ProSeed transforms reactive volatility detection into a proactive prediction mechanism. Instead of merely reacting to detected drift, it learns transitions between volatility patterns and drift detections to predict the timing and location of future drifts. By modeling these volatility trends as pattern transitions in a probabilistic network, ProSeed adapts its data compression strategy to focus detection efforts on predicted drift periods. Building on similar principles, ProChange (Koh et al., 2018) extends this volatility-based approach to transactional data streams, using probabilistic networks to predict drift locations and dynamically adapt confidence thresholds for both real and virtual concept drifts in unlabeled data. However, both approaches are specifically constrained to streams with recurring volatility patterns and require accurate volatility shift detection to build reliable predictive models, limiting their effectiveness in streams with other characteristics. Detect Abrupt Drift (DetectA) (Escovedo et al., 2018) takes a different approach by using unsupervised clustering to enable proactive drift detection. The method applies k-means clustering to unlabeled test data and statistical hypothesis tests before true labels arrive. DetectA’s proactive capability allows it to anticipate drift and either retrain the classifier with clustered labels or apply pattern mean shift corrections to incoming data. However, DetectA is specifically designed for abrupt drift scenarios, where sudden distributional changes make its statistical tests and pattern correction mechanisms more effective. These detection-focused proactive methods have paved the way for more sophisticated adaptation strategies. Nacre (Wu et al., 2021) addresses the challenge of reducing perfor mance loss between drift appearance and detection by leveraging concept recurrences in data streams. The framework proposes a novel drift coordinator that anticipates the next drift point and assesses the incoming concept to enable smooth adaptation. Nacre consists of three main components: a recurrent concept drift classifier, PEARL (Wu et al., 2020), which detects drift and models concepts, a drift sequence predictor that trains on sequences of drift intervals to predict future drift points, and the drift coordinator that orchestrates their interaction to perform both proactive drift detection and online updates. The key innovation lies in using drift sequences to anticipate future changes, allowing the system to be more proactive rather than purely reactive. However, Nacre assumes the existence of patterns in drift intervals and concept recurrence, requiring availability of labeled data and sufficient historical drift sequences to build reliable prediction models. Beyond the classification paradigm, proactive adaptation has also been applied to data stream processing tasks. For instance, Stream Window Join (SWJ) operations, which involve joining data streams with disordered arrival times (also referred to as stream oscil lation), present significant challenges. To address this, Proactive Error Compensation-Join (PECJ) (Zeng et al., 2024) was proposed as a proactive solution that accounts for anticipated future contributions from delayed data, thereby improving join accuracy. In the domain of time series forecasting, Proceed (PROaCtivE modEl aDaptation) (Zhao and Shen, 2024) represents one of the most recent advancements in proactive drift adap tation. Unlike ProSeed, which focuses on drift detection, Proceed is designed for proac tive adaptation, aligning closely with the objectives of our work. Proceed addresses delays in obtaining ground-truth labels, which can lead to models adapting to past and outdated concepts rather than current distributions. The key idea behind Proceed is that changes in the data distribution correspond to modifications in model parameters, meaning that con cept drift can be anticipated by learning a mapping from the concept space to the model’s parameter space. By combining this novel perspective with drift estimation techniques, Proceed enables models to proactively adjust their parameters before the new concept fully manifests. Furthermore, Zhao and Shen ( 2024) introduces a formal definition of online model adaptation, which aligns with the definitions of proactivity and reactivity discussed in the next section. Accordingly, we expand upon this definition to provide a more rigorous and generalized framework for understanding proactive and reactive adaptation in data stream learning.

## 3 Reactive and Proactive Concept Drift Adaptation

The terms proactivity and reactivity are commonly used to describe behavioral patterns in living beings. Proactivity refers to the ability to anticipate future events and take preventive actions to either avoid potential problems or make informed decisions in advance, ensuring preparation and mitigating possible consequences. Conversely, reactivity describes behav ior in which actions are taken only after a problem has already arisen, responding to its consequences rather than anticipating them. Both terms are fundamentally related to time and anticipation, as reactive behavior addresses problems after they have materialized, whereas proactive behavior seeks to prevent them. In the context of data streams, these concepts have been adopted to characterize different approaches to handling concept drift. The term reactive has been used to describe method ologies that respond to concept drift only after they have already manifested (Khamassi et al., 2018; Ditzler et al., 2015; Han et al., 2022). Similarly, the term proactive has been used for methodologies that attempt to anticipate concept drift or that continuously update model parameters (Gama et al., 2014; Gomes et al., 2017a). However, despite their widespread use in the literature, there exists no formal definition for these concepts in the concept drift adaptation domain. To address this gap, we propose formal mathematical definitions for both reactivity and proactivity in concept drift adaptation. These definitions serve as fundamental criteria for distinguishing between adaptation paradigms and provide a clearer framework for under standing how methods handle distributional changes. Before defining reactive and proactive adaptation strategies, we must establish a funda mental criterion that distinguishes between these paradigms. This criterion is based on the concept of model outdatedness. A model M is considered outdated at a given time step t if it has not yet adapted to an ongoing concept drift. In other words, the model parameters at time t, denoted as Θt, remain aligned with a previous data distribution Dk−1 rather than the current distribution Dk. Such outdatedness manifests as a significant performance degradation in predictive accuracy, which can be quantified using an error function L(·). Formally, if concept drift is present from time t′ onward, the model is considered outdated if the following condition is satisfied: |L(Mt′ ,S t′ ) −L (M t′ −1,S t′ −1)|≥ ϵ (2) where ϵ is a sensitivity threshold, Mt′ and Mt′ −1 represent the models with parameters Θt′ and Θt′ −1 respectively, and St′ and St′ −1 denote sets of instances drawn from distributions Dk′ and Dk′ −1 respectively. Note that we deliberately distinguish indices for instance arrival time steps (t′) from indices for concept distributions (k′), as these may not coincide in practice. This definition of outdatedness is crucial because it establishes the baseline condition that separates reactive approaches (which allow models to become outdated) from proactive approaches (which aim to prevent outdatedness).

### 3.1 Reactive Adaptation

We can define a model M as reactive if its adaptation mechanism is designed to update the model only after it has become outdated due to concept drift. In other words, it remains outdated for a certain period after concept drift starts, only adapting once its outdated state is detected. Reactive models fundamentally operate on a problem-detection paradigm: they identify when current performance or structure is suboptimal and then adapt accordingly. Formally, given that concept drift starts at t′, a reactive adaptation mechanism is trig gered at time t′ + K , with K ∈ N+, upon detecting the outdated state of the model, leading to an adjustment to recover from it: M t′ +K = Are(Mt′ +K −1,S t′ +K ), (3) where Are represents the reactive adaptation mechanism. Reactive models exhibit a common behavior: they wait until outdatedness is recog nized before adjusting. This paradigm encompasses both explicit and implicit adaptation approaches from traditional taxonomies, with some exceptions, as both fundamentally respond to manifested problems rather than prevent them. Most explicit approaches (excluding those using proactive detectors such as DetectA or ProSeed) rely on external drift detection mechanisms like DDM or ADWIN that signal when distribution changes occur, subsequently triggering model adaptation. These methods separate the detection phase from the adaptation response, operating with a delay between the onset of concept drift and the recognition of model outdatedness. This makes most explicit approaches reactive, as they can only adapt after the drift has manifested and been detected. This reactive nature is particularly evident in error-based detectors, which explicitly require performance degradation to occur before signaling adaptation needs. For example, ensemble methods like Adaptive Random Forest use dedicated detectors to identify when outdated trees should be replaced. Most implicit approaches integrate detection and adaptation within the learning pro cess itself. CVFDT exemplifies this reactive behavior by monitoring statistical confidence through alternate subtrees and replacing tree structures only when these alternatives demonstrate superior performance on recent data; it can only adapt after sufficient evidence of current structure inadequacy has been accumulated. Similarly, VFDT exhibits reactive learning behavior by waiting for sufficient statistical evidence before making structural decisions.

The defining characteristic that unites all reactive models is that they respond to detected problems rather than prevent them. Whether through external detectors or integrated learning mechanisms, they do not anticipate distributional changes but instead attempt to recover from outdatedness after concept drift has already affected their performance.

### 3.2 Proactive Adaptation

A model M is proactive if its adaptation mechanism is designed to mitigate or avoid the negative performance consequences of an incoming concept drift, i.e, prevent a future out dated state. Proactive models fundamentally operate on a prevention paradigm: they anticipate potential distribution changes and adjust their parameters accordingly before becoming outdated. Formally, given a concept drift starting at time t′, a proactive model performs an adaptation step at t′ − α ≥ 0: M t′ −α = Apro(Mt′ −α−1 ,S t′ −α ). (4)

Strictly speaking, our categorization depends on the nature of this adaptation mechanism. In practice, a model may employ multiple adaptation components with different characteristics, some reactive and others proactive. By conven tion, we classify a model as proactive when at least one of its adaptation mechanisms operates proactively Proactive methods can leverage various sources of information for anticipation, includ ing historical concept patterns (e.g., RePro using Markov chains), current distributional trends (e.g., Proceed mapping distribution changes to parameter space), or even structural patterns in incoming unlabeled data (e.g., DetectA). Regardless of the information source, the key characteristic is that these methods attempt to adapt before performance degradation occurs. Proactive adaptation strategies can be broadly categorized into two main approaches: 1. Algorithm-level proactive adaptation: These methods modify internal model parameters or structure directly based on anticipated changes. For example, Proceed proac tively modifies model parameters by learning a mapping from distributional changes to the parameter space, enabling direct parameter adjustment before new concepts fully manifest. 2. Data-level proactive adaptation: These methods leverage instances from the stream itself to guide the learning process, allowing the classifier to anticipate upcoming changes in the data distribution. This includes techniques such as generating synthetic instances that simulate future conditions, reusing old samples from previous concepts, or selectively reweighting recent observations to guide the model toward anticipated changes.

### 3.3 Drift Type Considerations and Limitations

The effectiveness of proactive adaptation is strongly influenced by the characteristics of concept drift. Certain types of concept drifts, such as virtual concept drifts, do not require any adaptation as they do not influence the decision boundaries learned by the classifier. This means that no degradation in performance is observed, which, in terms of our defini tions, implies that even if a virtual concept drift occurs, the model remains up-to-date. When considering different drift patterns, acyclic abrupt concept drifts present unique challenges since there is no feasible way to anticipate them through historical patterns. In contrast, abrupt but cyclic concept drifts may exhibit trends in the history of concept drifts that allow predicting their occurrence, enabling proactive adaptation. However, when dealing with acyclic and abrupt drifts, models must rely primarily on their reactive adaptation components. It is notable that DetectA can proactively detect abrupt concept drifts, but this is achieved by working with unlabeled input data before their ground-truth labels arrive rather than predicting future drift manifestation. While proactive adaptation aims to prevent performance degradation before outdated ness occurs, its effectiveness depends critically on the accuracy of future estimations. ## 4 Proposed Proactive Approaches

In this section we present four proactive approaches that are based on different proactive adaptation strategies. In order to implement these variations, we used Very Fast Decision Tree (VFDT) (Domingos and Hulten, 2000) as our base classifier. Domingos and Hulten introduced VFDT as one of the first algorithms for incrementally constructing decision trees from data streams under the assumption of a stationary distri bution (Domingos and Hulten, 2000). Although VFDT by itself does not adapt to concept drift, it incrementally updates its structure as more data arrive, which can modify parts of the tree when the distribution changes (Gama et al., 2014). In practice, effective drift adaptation usually requires augmenting VFDT with explicit mechanisms such as sliding windows, adaptive pruning, or drift detectors (e.g., CVFDT, HAT). We selected VFDT as our base model due to its modular and interpretable architecture and its strong empirically proven performance, making it well suited to apply controlled model adjustments (Pfahringer et al., 2007; Ikonomovska et al., 2011). Additionally, its simple but effective algorithm led to its use as a building block in numerous adaptive and ensemble-based models in the literature (Gomes et al., 2017a; Krawczyk et al., 2017). This combination of robustness, reactivity, and flexibility makes VFDT an excellent candidate to evaluate the potential of different proactive strategies. For our experimentation, we used the implementation of VFDT in the River library (Montiel et al., 2021), keeping all default hyperparameters except for the leaf prediction mechanism, which was modified to majority class one to ensure visually consistent output results.

### 4.1 Proactive Hoeffding Tree

VFDT provides a strong reactive baseline, adapting only after concept drift has begun and the model’s decision boundaries have already been affected. To explore the potential ben efits of proactive adaptation, we introduce a set of proactive strategies applied to VFDT, collectively referred to as the Proactive Hoeffding Tree (PHT). We propose four variations of PHT, each one incrementally incorporating proactive mechanisms. All variations share a window-based adaptation process, where adjustments are executed based on the most recent window of data, reinforcing the influence of recent observations to proactively anticipate concept drift (Lu et al., 2018). During our experi mentation, we used a window size | W| of 1000 samples, allowing proactive adjustments to be performed without excessive delay. This size was chosen considering the speed of the concept drifts studied in our experimentation. Additionally, in all proactive strategies presented in the following subsections, we employ a consistent notion of distance to study the trajectories of data samples in relation to a given decision boundary learnt by the model. A decision boundary is defined by a feature–threshold pair (f, τ ), and the distance of a sample x to the boundary is computed as the absolute difference |xf − τ|. Each sample is assigned to one of the two regions induced by the boundary (xf ≤ τ or xf >τ ). We identify the closest points as the samples with the smallest distances in each region, subject to a predefined per-region quota, ensuring a bal anced selection of reference points on both sides of the boundary. This consistent distance definition is applied across all upcoming proactive approaches.

#### 4.1.1 Proactive Hoeffding Tree Move

The concept of local adaptation in decision trees has been widely studied in the literature, mainly due to their flexible architecture, which allows for dynamic modifications at the node level (Lu et al., 2018; Ikonomovska et al., 2011). Proactive Hoeffding Tree Move (PHT-M) builds upon the VFDT, leveraging its malleable architecture, by introducing an enhanced yet simple mechanism for updating decision boundaries which aims to mitigate the impact of incremental concept drift by adjusting decision boundaries dynamically. The mechanism adapts the threshold, which remains fixed in the standard VFDT, at split nodes by analyzing the most recent |W| observations. This strategy can be classified as an algorithm-level proactive adaptation mechanism, as it modifies the representation that VFDT uses to define decision boundaries (feature-threshold pair). By dynamically adjust ing these thresholds based on recent data samples, PHT-M anticipates and adapts to gradual concept drifts before classification performance significantly deteriorates. Figure 3 presents how PHT-M proactive adaptation mechanisms works in two dimensional case. For a given window of points, the centroid of the closest points in each region is computed. The decision threshold is then updated to the mean feature value of these centroids, allowing the model to anticipate to incremental concept drifts. The threshold adaptation in PHT-M can be divided in the following steps. First, the algorithm maintains a sliding window of the most recent | W| observations, where | W| is a predefined parameter. Every | W| samples the proactive adjustment is performed. Second, for each decision boundary, feature-threshold pair (f, t) in the decision tree, the mechanism selects the closest points from among those that, when traversing the tree, reach the leaves under the decision node where the boundary, ( f, t), is defined. That is, instead of consider ing all points in the current window, only those that fall into the corresponding leaves are eligible for selection. The adaptation mechanism described above enables PHT-M to proactively anticipate gradual concept drifts by adjusting decision boundaries based on the data distribution changes rather than reacting to performance degradation (consequence of the VFDT learn ing process). This characteristic directly aligns with the definition of proactive adaptation. The proposed adaptation algorithm can also be interpreted as a margin maximization approach. In a binary classification problem, the ideal decision boundary should lie in an area of low data density, maximizing the margin between classes (Cao et al., 2006). By computing the centroid of points for the given feature on either side of the decision boundary, PHT-M approximates an optimal hyperplane between the two subset of closest points, R1 and R2. Focusing exclusively on points near the decision boundary provides an optimal balance between computational efficiency, adaptation relevance (prioritizing closest points as most informative samples), and stability (preventing drastic decision boundaries modifications due to outliers).

#### 4.1.2 Proactive Hoeffding Tree Move and Rotate

Similarly to the previous proactive approach, Proactive Hoeffding Tree Move and Rotate (PHT-MR) extends VFDT by introducing an adapting mechanism not only for thresholds of the decision nodes, but also for the features used in split nodes. This allows PHT-MR to rotate the feature-threshold hyperplane, redefining the decision boundaries in response to changes in data distribution. In certain cases, when the distribution of points in the input space evolves due to incre mental concept drift, a simple adjustment to the threshold of the split node is insufficient. The decision boundary may become misaligned with the optimal and intuitively expected deci sion hyperplane, what can be solved by modifying the orientation of the decision boundary. Figure 4 illustrates the proactive adaptation mechanism of PHT-MR in the two-dimen sional case, where a rotation of the decision boundary is executed. This rotation is per formed according to the angle, θ, between the centroids of the closest points to the decision boundary in each region. If the angle is below a predefined threshold, the decision boundary is rotated to the feature selected by a heuristic score (excluding the current one). In 2D input space, the rotation is performed to the unique possible feature. VFDT waits until sufficient statistical evidence is collected to replace a leaf node by a split node, resulting in the cre ation of an entirely new decision boundary, even when a simple adjustment to the existing one could have sufficed. PHT-MR addresses this inefficiency by introducing a straightfor ward but effective technique to rotate decision boundaries whenever such situations can be estimated. This is achieved by analyzing the angle formed between the centroids of the closest points on either side of the decision boundary and the decision boundary itself. Following the same principles as PHT-M, this approach aims to position the decision boundary in an optimal region, maximizing the separation between the closest points on both sides. The rotation mechanism in PHT-MR further enhances its ability to anticipate complex scenarios where the current decision boundary becomes outdated. Two key aspects of this approach are worth highlighting. First, the heuristic selected to determine the best feature for performing the rotation is the variance. Variance provides insight into which feature may offer the greatest separation between data points, although this might not always be true. The information gain heuristic, used by VFDT River’s default implementation, was discarded because of the suboptimal performance obtained during the experimentation. However, these heuristics can be adapted or replaced depending on the characteristics of the stream analyzed. Second, when a rotation is performed, a temporal threshold is initially assigned to the newly selected feature. This is necessary because the threshold of the previous feature is no longer valid. Therefore, a temporary estimation is made until sufficient observations allow for a more stable and well-adjusted threshold value. Since the rotation condition is based on the angle between the centroids and the decision boundary being below a certain threshold, the temporal threshold is estimated as the mean value of the previous computed centroids along the new feature. PHT-M and PHT-MR operate on a prevention paradigm rather than problem-detection. Unlike CVFDT, which waits for alternate subtrees to demonstrate superior performance before replacement, PHT-M and PHT-MR continuously optimize decision boundaries every |W| samples without waiting for performance degradation. The key distinction lies in their trigger mechanism: CVFDT responds to detected inadequacy by comparing competing structures, while PHT-M and PHT-MR proactively maximize class separability based on recent data patterns to prevent future outdatedness. This continuous optimization for robustness, rather than reactive replacement after detecting problems, exemplifies the prevention paradigm that defines proactive adaptation.

#### 4.1.3 Proactive Hoeffding Tree Move and Create

Proactive Hoeffding Tree Move and Create (PHT-MC) incorporates the same proactive threshold adaptation mechanism as PHT-M but extends it to anticipate more complex situ ations. This approach estimates whether a leaf node should be replaced by a subtree to anticipate a possible concept drift that could affect the decision boundary its parent split node represents. The subtree is constructed manually, following specific strategies to ensure consistency and mitigate the consequences of the incremental concept drift. Only then it would create a second new decision boundary that would finally classify adequately. In contrast, PHT-MC aims to address this issue by constructing the two-level subtree in a single step, by estimating the trajectory of the gradual concept drift, and analyzing the distribution of points within the blue region. After applying the same threshold adaptation mechanism that PHT-M uses, the novelty of PHT-MC is introduced. At every | W| samples, for each leaf in the tree, its history is updated. The history of a leaf consists of past centroids of the closest points to its parent’s decision boundary that were classified by the leaf. This history information is updated with the most recent window of observations. Then, by using the accumulated history informa tion for each leaf, the algorithm estimates whether the centroid of the samples classified by a leaf in the next window will move too close to the decision boundary. This means a potential situation for performance degradation may occur. Right after, if a potential performance degradation is detected, the approach builds a complete subtree to replace the sibling node of the leaf. Note that PHT-MC replace the sibling node of the one that is being analyzed, as the performance degradation will be suffered in that sibling leaf node of the tree due to the concept drift (points are moving towards the region classified by the sibling leaf node). To determine the appropriate structure of the subtree, PHT-MC analyzes the relationship between the centroid of the closest points to the parent’s decision boundary and all the window samples classified by the sibling leaf. #### 4.1.4 Proactive Hoeffding Tree Shadow

Proactive Hoeffding Tree Shadow (PHT-S) is the last proactive adaptation mechanism introduced to modify the standard VFDT, aiming to anticipate to gradual concept drifts. This approach is presented last due to its distinct nature; while previous approaches focused on modifying parameters learned by the VFDT (algorithm-level adaptation), PHT-S rely sol idly on VFDT’s mechanism while allowing some degree of proactivity. Unlike other approaches, PHT-S does not update the threshold or the feature of node splits. Instead, its adaptation mechanism is similar to that used by PHT-MC when creating a new subtree to replace existing leaf nodes. However, the key difference between PHT-S and PHT-MC lies in how these subtrees are created. Instead of manually constructing the subtree based on observed samples, PHT-S trains a new subtree (called shadow tree) by using semi-synthetic data points. This proactive training process occurs when the proximity of the data points to a decision boundary suggests an imminent change. In such cases, a shadow tree is trained using criti cal points from the potentially invaded region and synthetic ones, which are generated by estimating the future movement of the data points. This estimation is based on a history of previous distribution’s states, which allows the algorithm to simulate the likely structure of future windows. Once the estimated trajectory suggests that the new data will cross the decision boundary in the next iteration of the proactive mechanism, the shadow tree replaces the potentially invaded leaf node, mitigating the performance consequences of the concept drift. The algorithm maintains a sliding window of the most recent W observations, where W is a predefined parameter. Every W samples, the proactive adjustment is performed. Then, at each iteration, the history information is updated by storing previous states of the sample distribution. The history allows the model to estimate future trends based on recent changes. After updating the history, the algorithm checks whether the centroids of the closest points to a decision boundary (for a given split node) have entered a danger region, defined by the threshold movement ( τ ) hyperparameter. As in PHT-MC, if any centroid falls within this region, it is considered a potential risk for the current decision boundary, and a “shadow” tree (Hoeffding Tree classifier) is created if it has not been previously initialized. The shadow tree is designed to replace the leaf node that represents the region predicted to be invaded. If the shadow tree already exists for a given leaf node, it is incrementally trained with all the samples from the current window that have been classified by that leaf. Addi tionally, the algorithm identifies invader points, which are the closest points to the decision boundary that might cross it in the near future. These invader points are artificially shifted using an adjustment vector, computed based on the history information collected, illustrated in Fig. 7. The shadow tree is then trained using this adjusted dataset, combining the invader points with the current samples classified by the leaf. Finally, when the model estimates that the points will cross the decision boundary in the next data window, the leaf predicted to be invaded is replaced by the trained shadow tree.

This data-level approach enables us to leverage VFDT’s reactive mechanism in a proactive way without modifying its parameters. Instead, we identify critical points near the decision boundary and estimate their future positions, focusing on training the shadow tree to anticipate future changes in the data distribution. When the situation is critical, the shadow tree is used to mitigate the performance degradation that occurs when gradual concept drifts affect deci sion boundaries. Unlike the other proactive strategies described above, PHT-S minimizes reliance on heuristics. The only estimation performed is to generate synthetic points, resulting in a more natural and compatible adaptation mechanism with VFDT.

### 4.2 Linear Trajectory Assumptions and Method Constraints

The proposed PHT methods are fundamentally constrained by the linear nature of decision boundary adjustments in Hoeffding Trees. While the algorithms analyze the closest points to decision boundaries to anticipate distributional changes, the available transformations for axis-aligned hyperplanes are linear: threshold movement (PHT-M), rotation to differ ent features (PHT-MR), or creation of sub-hyperplanes (PHT-MC/PHT-S). These linear transformations can effectively accommodate distributional changes that align with geo metric patterns, even when the underlying cluster movements are curved, as the hyperplane adjustments can still provide adequate approximations. However, the proposed methods may struggle when concept drift involves non-geometric patterns or overly complex geo metric changes that cannot be adequately approximated through simple linear boundary transformations. The fundamental limitation emerges when distributional changes are either non-geometric in nature or too complex to be effectively approximated by linear hyperplane adjustments. As mentioned in Sect. 2, various proactive algorithms exist that do not assume linear adjustments since they are not based on decision trees. Methods such as RePro or Proceed, could be more effective proactive strategies when dealing with drift conditions that do not represent geometric patterns suitable for linear hyperplane transformations.

The results indicate that lower threshold values (less sensitive triggering) yield better per formance, with τ =0 .001 achieving the highest prequential accuracy (0.9579). This sug gests that conservative subtree creation may be preferable to avoid premature adaptions, even though performance differences are minimal (less than 0.003 in prequential accuracy across all tested thresholds). These results reinforce the robustness of the proposed proactive approaches. Despite being sensitive to different stream configurations, they demonstrate stability when deal ing with different hyperparameter values, as the performance differences remain statisti cally insignificant. However, as observed in both PHT-S and PHT-MC analyses, the optimal approach would be to develop adaptive hyperparameter mechanisms that dynamically adjust these thresholds based on stream characteristics. Such adaptive strategies could potentially enhance performance by automatically tuning parameters according to drift speed, com plexity, and other stream properties, representing a promising direction for future research.

### 6.3 Real-World Dataset Overall Analysis

An additional study using real-world datasets helps us understand the suitability of the proposed proactive approaches under uncertain drift conditions that may not follow linear assumptions. These experiments serve as a complex challenge for our approaches, whose proactive adaptation mechanisms are designed for incremental concept drifts with trajec tories approximable by linear transformations of the decision boundaries. In this context, we expect our proactive approaches to struggle to maintain their advantage against VFDT when faced with irregular, non-linear and chaotic drift patterns characteristic of real-world scenarios. Due to the nature of the streams selected, we have fixed |W | = 500 to allow more frequent proactive adjustments as most of the considered real-world streams were particularly short. Table 14 presents the aggregated results across all real-world datasets, revealing a different performance landscape compared to our synthetic experiments. The performance advantages of proactive approaches are significantly diminished in real-world scenarios, aligning with our expectations. However, the performance gap between the different approaches is not significant, obtaining all the approaches similar results. Interestingly, PHT-S obtains the highest results in all the metrics considered. This can be explained by its synthetic data generation methodology which allows the decision tree to leverage training on additional samples that approximate real data stream trends. The performance drop of PHT-M and PHT-MC, which dominated synthetic scenarios, is expected as these algorithms rely heavily on the linear nature of the distribution changes. The uncertain nature of real-world streams makes these approaches underperform in comparison to previous results. These findings highlight an important limitation of current trajectory-based proactive approaches and underscore the need for more proactive adaptive mechanisms that can handle non-linear drift patterns. ## 7 Limitations and Future Directions

While our comprehensive evaluation demonstrates the effectiveness of proactive adaptation strategies, several important limitations must be acknowledged. Most notably, proactive approaches rely fundamentally on the assumption that concept drift can be anticipated to some degree based on historical patterns or trends, limiting their applicability to scenarios where drift follows predictable trajectories. As such, they are generally ineffective in the presence of abrupt or specially complex distribution changes, where reactive mechanisms must assume full responsibility for adaptation. We have focused our experimentation on incremental concept drifts, as the sudden nature of abrupt concept drifts provides minimal opportunities for anticipation using the trajectorybased estimation methods presented, as the results shown. However, we acknowledge that some proactive approaches not explored in this work, such as those based in meta-learning, transfer learning, or even hybrid proactive-reactive methods (e.g. DetectA), may offer via ble solutions. Another significant challenge emerges in high-speed data streams, where concept drift evolves rapidly and instances arrive at a high rate. In such environments, fixed-size proac tive adaptation windows may become obsolete quickly, requiring more frequent updates using smaller windows that reduce the information available for reliable trajectory estima tion. Our evaluation also revealed that certain proactive strategies may perform exception ally well under specific conditions but fail entirely in others. While methods like PHT-S or PHT-MC showed some dependence on hyperparameters, they produced robust results without significant differences, even though these parameter-sensitive approaches could benefit from self-tunning strategies. Based on our evaluation, we strongly recommend the application of proactive adaptation strategies in scenarios characterized by gradual and minimally predictable concept drifts, where they consistently outperformed reactive baselines. ## 8 Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a novel categorization of adaptation mechanisms for handling concept drift, distinguishing between proactive and reactive strategies in non-stationary data streams. While these terms have been occasionally used to describe models, to the best of our knowledge, they had not been rigorously defined or systematically analyzed before.

We presented and evaluated several proactive adaptation mechanisms built upon the VFDT classifier, providing a comprehensive overview of their potential. Our study primarily addresses incremental concept drifts, as this type of drift allows for the possibility of anticipation, although we extended our experimentation to include varying incremental drift speeds and abrupt concept drifts scenarios. We evaluated these approaches across diverse synthetic data streams and real-world datasets, providing valuable insights into how proactive mechanisms can effectively address challenges that impact well-established baselines like VFDT. Our findings demonstrate the importance of proactivity in mitigating the negative impact of concept drift on classification performance. These findings also highlight the absence of proactive strategies in the literature. At the same time, we acknowledge several limitations of proactive strategies: they rely on assumptions or estimations about data stream trends, which may not be always accurate, and they may exhibit instability under certain condi tions. Therefore, we argue that proactive adaptation deserves further attention but should be carefully analyzed to properly address the concept drift on certain data stream settings. As future work, we plan to explore the integration of proactive strategies into stateof-the-art classifiers and ensembles methods, which have shown superior performance in the data stream learning. Additionally, we aim to design a novel classifier with an implicit proactive adaptation mechanism, capable of handling real-world concept drifts by anticipating changes in the data distribution, building on the promising capabilities shown by the approaches studied in this work.
